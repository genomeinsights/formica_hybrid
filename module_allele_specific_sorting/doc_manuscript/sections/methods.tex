\subsection{Rationale}

A locus is strongly differentiated when its allele frequency varies among populations. Two loci show among-population LD only when their frequencies vary across the \emph{same} populations in a consistent direction. Strong differentiation can therefore accumulate across the genome without strong multilocus LD if ancestry has sorted differently among loci. We refer to this as locus-specific ancestry sorting.

\subsection{Units, populations and orientation}

We analysed 165 individuals from 20 hybrid populations. The distance analysis used 20,807 LD-reduced units representing 51,612 ancestry-informative SNPs (parental diagnostic index $>-25$; hybrids-only LD clustering with a decay-relative quality gate $\rho=0.5$ and a 5~cM merge cap). Combining correlated SNPs avoids counting one genomic region repeatedly but removes some short-range LD, so the neighbourhood analysis was repeated with all 51,612 unpruned SNPs. Genotypes were oriented so that dosage counts the \Fa\ allele, identified from the parental reference samples. For each marker we calculated its \Fa-allele frequency in every population and estimated differentiation among populations using the \citet{weir1984estimating} \FST. Using the project-wide sorting criteria (near-fixation $\phi=0.85$, sorting threshold $\tau=0.6$, binomial $\alpha=0.05$), 1,215 units were sorted toward \Fa\ and 365 toward \Fp.

\subsection{Pairwise statistics}

\paragraph{Ancestry-profile similarity.} For each pair of markers $i$ and $j$, ancestry-profile similarity $c_{ij}$ is the correlation of their oriented allele-frequency profiles across the 20 populations. A positive value means that the same populations were enriched for \Fa\ ancestry at both loci. We repeated the calculation after removing each population's genome-wide ancestry, estimated from all other chromosomes.

\paragraph{Among-population LD and its differentiation ceiling.} Two loci are in LD among populations when their allele frequencies rise and fall across the same populations. Following the decomposition of LD in subdivided populations by \citet{ohta1982linkage}, this among-population component, normalised as a correlation, is
\begin{equation}
r_{ST,ij}=\frac{\operatorname{cov}_k(F_{ik},F_{jk})}{\sqrt{\bar F_i(1-\bar F_i)\,\bar F_j(1-\bar F_j)}}=c_{ij}\sqrt{G_iG_j},
\qquad G_i=\frac{\operatorname{var}_k(F_{ik})}{\bar F_i(1-\bar F_i)},
\label{eq:rst}
\end{equation}
where $G_i$ is the among-population differentiation of locus $i$ (an unweighted, Nei-type measure computed from population frequencies, distinct from the Weir--Cockerham \FST\ used elsewhere). Equation~\eqref{eq:rst} shows that among-population LD has two separate ingredients: how strongly each locus is differentiated ($G_i$, $G_j$), and whether the two loci differentiate the \emph{same} populations in the same direction ($c_{ij}$). Differentiation alone is not enough. Two highly differentiated loci produce strong among-population LD only if their population profiles agree; if each separates a different subset of populations, $c_{ij}$ is close to zero and so is $r_{ST,ij}$, however high their \FST.

The product $G_iG_j$ is therefore a \emph{ceiling}: the largest $r_{ST,ij}^2$ the two loci could show given their differentiation, reached only if they partition the populations identically ($|c_{ij}|=1$). For each distance class (all cross-chromosome pairs forming the ``unlinked'' class) we summarised how much of this ceiling is realised as
\begin{equation}
R=\frac{\sum r_{ST}^2}{\sum G_iG_j}=\frac{\sum G_iG_j\,c_{ij}^2}{\sum G_iG_j},
\label{eq:realised}
\end{equation}
the differentiation-weighted mean of $c_{ij}^2$. A high $R$ means that differentiated loci share their population partition, so that their differentiation is translated into among-population LD. A shared partition can arise from linkage (nearby loci share their history), from genome-wide population structure (populations differ in overall ancestry, so that every ancestry-informative locus tracks the same gradient), or from selection favouring particular combinations of alleles. A low $R$ means that loci are strongly differentiated but distinguish different populations, so that high differentiation does not raise LD: the signature of locus-specific sorting.

\paragraph{Permutation baseline and the share of the ceiling realised.} $R$ is not zero for unrelated loci: with only 20 populations, two independent profiles have an expected squared correlation of $1/19\approx0.053$. We estimated this baseline, $b$, for each distance class by permuting population labels independently for every unit (500 permutations). This preserves each unit's profile and differentiation, and therefore the ceiling, while removing any correspondence between loci. For cross-chromosome pairs (206.8 million) the permuted sums were computed exactly from per-chromosome $20\times20$ matrices rather than pair by pair. To express the realised fraction on an interpretable scale we rescaled it against the baseline,
\begin{equation}
S=\frac{R-b}{1-b},
\label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
the share of the ceiling, beyond chance, that is realised: $S=0$ when loci share their population partitions no more than unrelated loci would, and $S=1$ when all pairs partition the populations identically. Confidence intervals for $S$ were obtained by transforming the bootstrap interval of $R$ with $b$ fixed; the permutation interval of $b$ itself was negligible ($\pm10^{-5}$ for unlinked pairs).

\paragraph{Within-population LD.} Within-population LD was measured as the correlation between oriented genotypes of individuals from the same population, after removing the part explained by differences in genome-wide ancestry among individuals (estimated from all other chromosomes). We refer to this as ancestry-adjusted within-population LD; the adjustment ensures that two loci do not appear associated merely because some individuals carry more \Fa\ ancestry across their whole genome. Missing genotypes (2.7\%) were set to the population mean. Because sorted loci are near-fixed in many populations, comparisons of sorted and unsorted loci included only populations in which both markers segregated ($r_w^{\mathrm{poly}}$).

\subsection{Distance analysis}

We evaluated all 9.6 million within-chromosome pairs of LD-reduced units and all 206.8 million cross-chromosome pairs. Pairs were grouped by genetic distance, with physical distance used as a sensitivity analysis; cross-chromosome pairs formed an ``unlinked'' class. Pairs whose interpolated map positions were identical (21,886 within-chromosome pairs, typically 5--100~kb apart within map intervals without recorded recombination) have an undefined rather than a small genetic distance; they were excluded from the genetic-distance analysis but retained in the physical-distance analysis. Confidence intervals came from 2,000 chromosome-block bootstrap replicates.

\subsection{Neighbourhoods of sorted loci}

If sorting involved long haplotypes, markers near sorted loci should show more similar ancestry patterns and stronger within-population LD than markers near comparable unsorted loci. We matched each sorted locus to up to five unsorted controls based on \FST, local recombination rate, parental allele-frequency difference and local density of LD units (units within $\pm100$~kb). A 1-SD caliper retained 1,552 of 1,577 eligible sorted loci and achieved good balance on all covariates (absolute standardised mean differences $\leq0.04$); narrower calipers excluded many large sorted blocks without improving balance. The 25 unmatched loci are listed separately (Table~\ref{tab:unmatched}).

For each focal locus, we averaged ancestry-profile similarity and $r_w^{\mathrm{poly}}$ with neighbouring markers up to 2~Mb away. We did this both for other LD-reduced units and for all unpruned SNPs, including SNPs in the focal locus's own LD cluster. We also compared the number of SNPs and physical span of the focal LD units. Each sorted locus was compared with the mean of its own controls, and 2,000 chromosome-block bootstrap replicates kept these matched sets intact.
