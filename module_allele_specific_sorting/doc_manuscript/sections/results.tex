\subsection{High differentiation, but shared population partitions mainly over linkage distances}

Ancestry-informative units were strongly differentiated among the hybrid populations (median \FST\ $=0.25$). If every pair of loci had distinguished the same populations, this differentiation would have produced strong among-population LD throughout the genome. This maximum --- the among-population LD ceiling --- was therefore high, and nearly constant across distances, because it depends only on how differentiated each locus is (Figure~\ref{fig:decay}a).

In reality, pairs of loci fell well short of this ceiling. The \emph{realised fraction} --- the proportion of the ceiling actually observed --- measures how far differentiated loci distinguish the same populations in the same direction: it would be 1 if all loci shared one population pattern, and close to the chance level if each locus differentiated its own, unrelated set of populations. The realised fraction was low and declined rapidly with genetic distance (Figure~\ref{fig:decay}b), meaning that loci shared population patterns mainly when they were physically linked. The realised fraction fell from 0.217 [95\% CI 0.203, 0.233] at 0.001--0.01~cM to 0.065 at 1--5~cM and 0.0571 [0.0568, 0.0574] between chromosomes, close to the permutation baseline of 0.053. Rescaled against this baseline (Equation~\eqref{eq:share}), the share of the ceiling realised beyond chance was 17.3\% [15.9, 19.1] at 0.001--0.01~cM, 3.3\% at 0.2--1~cM, 1.3\% at 1--5~cM and 0.47\% [0.44, 0.50] between chromosomes. Even between tightly linked units, most of the among-population LD that their differentiation could produce was therefore not realised; between chromosomes, almost none was. The small excess between chromosomes reflected the genome-wide ancestry gradient among populations: ancestry-profile similarity between chromosomes was 0.033 [0.030, 0.036], but effectively zero after removing genome-wide ancestry ($-0.0004$ [$-0.0007$, $-0.0000$]). Within-population LD declined similarly, from $r_w^2=0.159$ at 0.001--0.01~cM to 0.025 at 1--5~cM and 0.008 between chromosomes.

Similarity and LD were equally high in the two shortest genetic-distance classes (realised fraction 0.213 [0.171, 0.279] below 0.001~cM and 0.217 [0.203, 0.233] at 0.001--0.01~cM) and declined monotonically beyond them; the physical-distance analysis, which also includes pairs without a resolvable genetic distance, showed the same monotonic decline (Figure~\ref{fig:decay-bp}).

Thus, the genome is strongly differentiated overall, but regions beyond the scale of linkage usually differentiate different populations. This declining profile shows that shared population patterns arise at the scale of linkage rather than from genome-wide population structure, which would contribute equally at all distances. It does not by itself distinguish selection from neutral processes, however: drift within populations also generates among-population LD between linked loci that decays with recombination \citep{ohta1982linkage}. Whether the observed level exceeds neutral expectations awaits comparison with the simulations.

\subsection{Sorted loci do not share their sorting with nearby markers more than matched unsorted loci}

The matched controls were closely balanced with the sorted loci on all matching covariates (Figure~\ref{fig:balance}). Across both marker sets, sorted loci showed no greater ancestry-profile similarity or within-population LD with nearby markers than their controls (Table~\ref{tab:neighbourhood}; Figure~\ref{fig:neighbourhood}).

Within 5~kb, ancestry-profile similarity was lower around sorted loci than around controls, both for LD-reduced units (differences $-0.057$ to $-0.073$) and for unpruned SNPs ($-0.061$ to $-0.073$). Differences were small at 5--20~kb and absent from 20~kb to 2~Mb. Within-population LD showed no positive excess at any distance; where differences were detectable, LD was slightly lower around sorted loci (LD-reduced units at 1--5~kb, $-0.029$ [$-0.052$, $-0.006$]; unpruned SNPs at 0--1~kb, $-0.027$ [$-0.058$, $-0.001$], and at 0.5--2~Mb, $-0.006$ [$-0.012$, $-0.002$]). The LD units containing sorted loci were no larger than those of their controls ($\log_2$ SNPs: 0.027 [$-0.044$, 0.088]) and spanned less physical distance (13 versus 26~kb; $-12.7$ [$-29.7$, $-0.1$]).

Because the unpruned analysis included SNPs within each focal locus's own LD cluster, it could directly detect extended haplotypes. Sorted loci do lie in larger LD blocks than unsorted loci in general (mean span 27 versus 4~kb), but this follows from their high differentiation and lower recombination, which their controls share. The absence of any excess relative to these controls shows that sorting is not associated with longer ancestry blocks than equally differentiated loci in comparable recombination contexts, consistent with locus-specific sorting. The 25 sorted loci without suitable controls remain possible exceptions (Table~\ref{tab:unmatched}): 11 lie in LD blocks spanning more than 100~kb (10 of them more than 500~kb), whereas 10 are single-SNP units with unusual covariate values; the matched analysis cannot test them. The lower short-range similarity around matched sorted loci may partly reflect the sorting definition itself, which requires near-fixation that an equally differentiated but unsorted neighbour lacks.

\subsection{Near-neutral loci share their (weak) differentiation more genome-wide}

Near-neutral loci were weakly differentiated (mean $G=0.044$, against 0.30 for ancestry-informative units), so their ceiling was about 20--90 times lower (Figure~\ref{fig:lowdi}a). Relative to this ceiling, their differentiation was organised differently from that of ancestry-informative loci (Figure~\ref{fig:lowdi}b). Over linkage distances they realised a smaller share of the ceiling beyond chance (0.05--0.2~cM: 0.018 [0.006, 0.035] versus 0.100 [0.094, 0.106]; 0.2--1~cM: 0.013 [0.010, 0.017] versus 0.033 [0.031, 0.036]), whereas between chromosomes they realised a larger share (0.0073 [0.0062, 0.0085] versus 0.0047 [0.0044, 0.0050]). This is the pattern expected if near-neutral differentiation reflects population history acting on all loci at once, whereas the differentiation of ancestry-informative loci is largely locus-specific and shared mainly through linkage. Pairs of near-neutral units closer than 0.05~cM were few (130--1,241 per class) and are not interpreted.

Within-population LD between near-neutral markers decayed to background by about 0.05~cM, whereas between ancestry-informative markers it extended to 1--5~cM (Figure~\ref{fig:lowdi}c). Markers that differ between the parental species also carry admixture LD from the ancestry tracts left by hybridisation, which is long-range because it is recent; markers that do not differ between the parents only reflect older, short-range LD from within the parental species. The distance over which ancestry-informative loci share their population patterns therefore matches the scale of ancestry tracts.

\subsection{A neutral model reproduces near-neutral but not ancestry-informative differentiation}

Across the whole simulation grid, neutral hybrid populations reached the differentiation of near-neutral loci but fell far short of that of ancestry-informative loci (Table~\ref{tab:neutralsim}, Figure~\ref{fig:neutralsim}). In the best-fitting simulation, near-neutral \FST\ was 0.033 against 0.031 empirically, whereas ancestry-informative \FST\ was 0.025 against 0.260; even the most differentiated simulation reached only 0.086. Within-population fixation showed the same contrast: 17.6\% of ancestry-informative unit $\times$ population combinations were monomorphic empirically, against at most 0.9\% in any simulation. The simulations also underestimated fixation at near-neutral loci (2.3\% empirically, at most 1.6\%), but by a much smaller factor. Under neutrality, ancestry-informative loci were never more differentiated than near-neutral loci, whereas empirically they were about eight times more differentiated. A neutral model that reproduces background differentiation therefore cannot account for the differentiation of ancestry-informative loci.

The simulations have clear limitations. Within-population LD at short distances was too high for both classes of loci (excess LD at 0.05--0.2~cM: 0.018--0.037 against 0.003 for near-neutral loci; 0.27--0.49 against 0.085 for ancestry-informative loci). Because the mismatch also affects near-neutral loci, it reflects the model rather than sorting, and LD-based statistics, including the realised share of the ceiling, were not compared with simulations; the evidence that sorting is locus-specific rests on the empirical contrasts above. The model also assumes a single founding event from one pool of parents; alternative histories, such as hybrid populations derived from an older, already admixed source, or populations founded from different parental sources, were not simulated. Finally, the simulations cannot distinguish selection against incompatible ancestry combinations from environmental adaptation.
