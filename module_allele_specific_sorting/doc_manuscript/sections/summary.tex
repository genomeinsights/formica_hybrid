Populations differed strongly in ancestry across much of the ancestry-informative genome, but different genomic regions usually distinguished different sets of populations. Nearby regions often showed similar ancestry patterns, as expected because they are linked. This similarity declined rapidly with genetic distance and was close to the finite-sample baseline for regions more than about 5~cM apart and for regions on different chromosomes; the small remaining excess for unlinked regions reflected the shared gradient in genome-wide ancestry among populations. Thus, strong differentiation across the genome did not arise because the same populations repeatedly carried the same ancestry at every differentiated region (Figure~\ref{fig:decay}).

Directionally sorted loci did not share their sorting with neighbouring markers more than similarly differentiated unsorted loci in comparable genomic contexts did. Within 5~kb they shared it somewhat less, and their own LD clusters were no larger. This held both for LD-reduced units and for all unpruned SNPs, including those inside the sorted locus's own LD cluster (Figure~\ref{fig:neighbourhood}). The result is consistent with ancestry having sorted largely region by region rather than through long, consistently inherited ancestry blocks. The exception is a small number of very large sorted LD blocks, typically in regions of lower recombination, which had no comparable unsorted region and could not be assessed in this way (Table~\ref{tab:unmatched}).

Overall, the results support a simple interpretation: differentiation among the hybrid populations is assembled from many genomic regions that have followed partly independent histories.
